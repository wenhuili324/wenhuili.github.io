Studying fluid behavior at interfaces is critical to understanding a wide range of processes in energy and environmental systems. At the nanometer scale, where interfacial phenomena such as molecular layering, orientation, and interfacial tension (IFT) emerge, experimental observation becomes extremely challenging—especially under harsh thermodynamic conditions like high pressure and temperature. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide a powerful and indispensable tool to probe these interfacial properties with atomic resolution, enabling insights that are otherwise difficult or impossible to obtain experimentally.

Understanding fluid structures and interactions at interfaces is essential for optimizing applications such as enhanced oil and gas recovery using chemical agents, controlling gas hydrate formation, demulsification, and managing multiphase flows in porous media. Accurate modeling of interfacial properties like IFT, adsorption behavior, and molecular organization can inform the design of more effective surfactants, inhibitors, and separation strategies. As such, MD simulations play a pivotal role in advancing our understanding of complex interfacial systems and improving performance in industrial applications.

In this project, we investigated how surfactants, co-surfactants, salt ions, and nanoparticles influence the structural and dynamic properties of hydrocarbon–water interfaces. Our findings highlight the synergistic role of co-surfactants in reducing interfacial tension and provide clarity on the debated effects of nanoparticles and ion concentrations. We also simulated water/oil/asphaltene interfaces under electric fields, revealing that field-induced disruption of asphaltene π–π stacking and adsorption can be stabilized by the presence of salt ions. Additionally, we explored the behavior of brine droplets in oil/asphaltene systems under electric fields using MD simulations. The results demonstrate that ion type, concentration, and asphaltene adsorption collectively govern droplet stability and migration, offering new insights into the mechanisms of electrostatic demulsification.
